The harbour was quiet that morning, and the boats lay still against the wall. An old man mended a net in the shade, and he did not look up when the bell rang across the water.

By noon the wind had turned. Fishermen walked down from the village in twos and threes, talking about the price of salt, the new road and a cousin who had gone abroad and never written.

Nobody could say when the lighthouse had last been painted. Its door stood open, its stairs smelled of oil, and a family of swallows had built a nest above the lamp without asking anyone.

"You will want a coat," said the woman at the inn, handing over a key. "It is warm now, but the evening comes in from the sea, and it does not knock first."

He climbed to the room, opened the window and counted the ships. There were seven, then six, then only the long grey line where the sky came down to meet the water and the day ended.

In the morning he wrote three letters. The first was to his brother, the second to a bank, and the third to somebody whose address he no longer knew, so he folded that one twice and kept it.
